Launch tracker

What is real, what only looks real, and what has to become real before strangers can rely on it. The launch plan gets you to a private beta in a fortnight; this page is the longer road behind it — the infrastructure the app currently fakes, the decisions still open inside the product, and the decisions outside it that no amount of code will settle. Read it once end to end, then work the checkboxes.

First, the honest summary

You have built far more than most first products ever reach: a complete, coherent, opinionated app with a real almanac engine, a real corpus, three scripts, offline reading and a design language of its own. What you have not built is a back end — and almost everything still missing is downstream of that one absence.

None of that is a mistake — it is exactly the right way to have got here. It is the wrong way to run a product with a thousand households on it. The distance between those two sentences is this document.

Three words, used precisely

Real
It works, and it will keep working for someone who isn't you
No stub behind it, no hand-waving. It may still have bugs; it does not have a hole.
Prototype
It looks finished and behaves finished — until conditions change
The dangerous category. A screen that works on your phone and loses everything on theirs. Most of what follows is here.
Missing
The seam exists; nothing is behind it
Honest, and therefore safe — as long as the UI admits it. The rule: a prototype must never look real to a user. Cut it, or label it.

The ladder — five stages, and where you stand

"Launch" is not one event. Naming the stages stops you from paying stage-four costs during stage two, which is how solo builders run out of energy before they run out of users.

0
Prototype — where you are today
Complete as an artefact, dependent on one device and one builder. Data lives in the browser; nothing recovers from a cleared cache.
1
Private beta — the next fortnight
Hosted, passcode-latched, 20–50 invited households. Stubs are allowed here if labelled. Purpose: find out whether people actually recite with it. Nothing else.
2
Foundation — the real work, 6–10 weeks
A back end exists. Accounts are real, data syncs and can be exported, the corpus loads on demand, payments actually take money, reminders arrive with the app closed. This is the stage that turns a prototype into a product, and it is the stage nobody sees.
3
Public launch — open doors
Anyone can install it. Which means: a privacy policy, a support address a human answers, an update path, and a corpus you would defend in front of a paṇḍita. Marketing is the least of it.
4
Sustaining — the part that decides whether it survives
Recorded recitation, the kids app, contributions from others, a rhythm of monthly releases, and enough money coming in to cover the bill without your salary. Do not design for this stage yet; do not architect against it either.
Rule of thumb for the whole ladder: a feature is not shipped until it survives someone else's phone, someone else's network and someone else's mistakes.

Storage, since you asked — the corpus as it grows

The right answer is unglamorous and cheap, and it is not a database. Here is the reasoning, because the reasoning generalises.

Where you are. ~70 texts across 40 JavaScript files, 1.7 MB, all loaded eagerly as globals before the app renders. At the full 127-title index that is roughly 3 MB; at 300 titles with three scripts and meanings, closer to 8–10 MB. A first open on an ordinary Indian mobile connection would take tens of seconds to show a single verse — and a reciter who wants one hymn has downloaded a thousand.

What hymn text actually is. Immutable, read-only, versioned rarely, identical for every user, and needed one document at a time. That is not database-shaped. That is file-shaped — the same shape as an image.

The recommendation. One JSON file per stotra, served as a static asset from a CDN, fetched on demand and cached on the device by the service worker. A small index file — title, deity, verse count, tags, script availability, proof status, content hash — ships with the app and powers library and search without touching a single text. First open drops to a few hundred kilobytes. A hymn you have read once opens instantly and works offline forever. Adding the 400th stotra costs one file and one index line, and changes nothing else.
What you do not need, and why. A relational database earns its keep on data you query, filter, join and mutate. You do none of that to a stotra. A database here buys you a monthly bill, a migration burden and a single point of failure in exchange for nothing — while a static file on a CDN is the most reliable, cheapest, fastest thing on the internet. If full-text search across the corpus outgrows the index, build a prebuilt search index as another static file before you reach for a search service.
What does need a database — and it is small: user accounts, their saved lists and prefs, the recitation ledger, gifts recorded against a name, push subscriptions, and feedback. Kilobytes per household, and all of it genuinely mutable. One managed Postgres, or one document store, or the store bundled with whichever platform you pick. Do not conflate the two problems; they have opposite shapes.
Audio, when it comes. Same logic, bigger numbers — a sahasranāma recitation is 20–60 MB. Static files on object storage behind a CDN, streamed, never bundled, never in the offline cache unless the user asks for that hymn by name. Budget bandwidth, not storage: storage is pennies, egress is the bill.
Define the stotra file format and freeze it
One JSON document per hymn: id, titles per language, author, source, proof status, content hash, verses with per-script lines, IAST, gloss. This is your data contract — the handoff bundle already calls the hymn shape the contract; write it down as a schema and version it from day one.
Stage 2

Write the converter once and keep it — you will re-run it every time the format changes. Never hand-edit sacred text twice.
Stage 2

Now

What must become real before public launch

Six items, in dependency order. Each is a stage-2 build, not a stage-1 one — but each needs its decision made now, because the decision shapes the port.

Pick one platform and let it answer four questions at once
Hosting, database, authentication and serverless functions. Choosing one integrated platform instead of four best-of-breed services is the single highest-leverage decision available to a solo builder — it removes four integrations you would otherwise maintain alone. Any of the mainstream managed platforms will do; the wrong choice here is no choice, or four choices.
Blocks all
Real accounts, with the lightest possible touch
Phone or Google sign-in, because this audience has both and remembers neither passwords nor email addresses. Critically: keep the app fully usable without an account — sign-in exists to carry practice across devices, never as a gate before the first verse. Migrate what is already in the browser on first sign-in rather than asking people to rebuild their lists.
Then everything
Sync and export for practice data
Local-first still — the app must work in airplane mode — with the server as the backup of record. Plus a one-tap export of the ledger. Users trust software that lets them leave with their data.
Trust-critical
Payments that actually settle — or none at all
Requires a registered entity, a bank account and KYC before a single rupee can move; that paperwork is weeks, not hours, so start it before you need it. Until it clears, the giving screen must say plainly that giving isn't open yet.
Long lead
Push, properly — server-scheduled, device-delivered
VAPID keys, a subscription store, a per-minute scheduler computing due cues from each subscriber's saved place, and a service-worker push handler. Already scoped in Pending; it needs the platform decision first. This is also the feature most likely to bring people back daily — treat it as a growth feature, not a nicety.
Retention
A real release process
Version control on everything, a staging URL that is not the live one, a build stamp visible in-app, cache-busted deploys, and the ability to roll back in minutes. Also: an error reporter, so you learn about crashes from software rather than from a disappointed relative.
Stage 2

Pending decisions — inside the product

Open questions the code cannot answer. Each carries my recommendation; disagree freely, but decide — an undecided question quietly blocks work in three other places.

Is there a paid tier, ever?
Today the app both meters printing and asks for dāna — two philosophies at once, and the meter contradicts the app's own stated stance that nothing is withheld. My recommendation: no paid tier. Dāna only, generously framed, with a lamp in the giver's name. Devotional audiences give more when they are not charged, and a paywall on a stotra invites a criticism you cannot win. Delete the print meter.
How wide is the corpus at launch, and where does it stop?
The sourcing tracker holds real scope questions — the full Durgā Saptaśatī, the ~352-verse Guru Gītā, Tamil Tiruppugazh "selections", texts in Telugu and Avadhi rather than Sanskrit. Recommendation: launch narrow and finished. Ship only proofed texts. Sixty impeccable stotras beat a hundred and twenty-seven with three errors, and each of those long recensions is a project of its own. Publish the roadmap so absence reads as deliberate.
Who has the authority to say a text is correct?
This is the deepest question in the product and it is not technical. Two named sources agreeing is a good bar for a beta; a named human reviewer is the bar for launch. Recommendation: find one or two paṇḍitas or senior reciters willing to be credited as reviewers, per deity if you can. Their names in the colophon are worth more than any feature you could build this quarter — and asking is free.
Which recension, when sources differ?
Needed as a written policy, not case by case. Recommendation: name a primary source per deity, follow it, and show the source and any known variant on the hymn's own page. Showing your work converts a potential complaint into evidence of care.
Accuracy claim for the pañcāṅga
People will plan real observances on it. Recommendation: state the method, the ayanāṃśa, the reference pañcāṅga you validated against, and the margin — and add a line telling anyone with a local tradition to follow their own. Humility here is both honest and protective.
Website, app, or both — and which is the front door?
You maintain a phone app, a tablet/desktop layout and a marketing site. Recommendation: one installable web app, and the site is one page whose only job is to get people to install it. Three surfaces is three times the testing for one builder.
Language of first contact
Devanāgarī, Telugu and IAST are all supported, but something must be shown before the user has chosen. Recommendation: ask once, in the first screen, in all three scripts at once — the choice itself signals who the app is for.
Can users contribute — texts, corrections, recordings?
The scaling answer eventually, and a governance problem immediately. Recommendation: not at launch. Accept corrections only, through one door, reviewed by you. Reopen the question when you have reviewers.

Pending decisions — outside the product

The part first-time builders discover late and at the worst moment. Several have lead times measured in weeks, so they belong on the calendar now even though none of them is code. I am a designer, not your lawyer or your accountant — treat the legal and tax lines as a list of questions to take to a professional, not as advice.

Under what name does Stuti exist?
You cannot take money, sign up for a payment gateway, or publish terms as an unnamed individual without accepting personal liability. Options, roughly: sole proprietorship (fast, cheap, personal liability), a private limited company (costlier, cleaner, investable), or a not-for-profit trust or Section 8 company (fits dāna and devotional work, opens donation-specific tax treatment, more paperwork). Recommendation: talk to a chartered accountant about the trust/Section 8 route before defaulting to a company — the app's giving model points that way. Longest lead time on this page. Start it this month.
Blocks money
Is the name yours, and is the domain?
Search the trademark register for "Stuti" in the relevant classes, register the domain and its obvious variants, and take the handle on every platform you might one day use. Cheap now; expensive and sometimes impossible after you have users.
Where does every text come from, and may you publish it?
The hymns themselves are ancient and free. Particular editions, translations, transliterations and glosses are not — a modern scholar's translation or a publisher's edited text is somebody's copyright, and "found on a stotra site" is not permission. Recommendation: record a source and a rights basis for every single text in your ledger, prefer public-domain or explicitly-open sources, and write your own glosses. This is the one risk on the page that could force you to take content down.
Legal
Fonts, and everything else you did not write
Check the licence of each bundled typeface for web-embedding rights, and of every library. Keep a one-page attributions file in the app. Ten minutes, and it closes a whole class of risk.
Privacy policy and terms — plain-language, and true
The moment you have accounts you are handling personal data, and India's DPDP Act brings real duties: stated purpose, consent, deletion on request, breach notification. Recommendation: write both documents in the app's own voice, list exactly what you store and why, and promise less than you technically could. Your analytics stance — count actions, never people — is already the right instinct; put it in writing.
Blocks public
Children, if the kids app ever ships
Products aimed at children carry a stricter regime nearly everywhere. Do not touch it without advice — another reason it is correctly out of scope.
Installable web app, or the app stores?
The stores buy discovery and credibility at the cost of a developer account, review cycles, store policies on religious content, and — if you ever charge — a platform commission on payments. Recommendation: launch as an installable web app; revisit the stores only when discovery, not delivery, is your bottleneck. Your users will find you through family, not search.
Who answers the mail, and how fast?
Pick a support address, put it in the app, and commit publicly to a turnaround you can actually keep — "within three days" honoured beats "immediately" missed. Decide your own hours before your users decide them for you. Solo burnout is the most common cause of death for products like this.
Who are the first hundred households, and who introduces you?
Not a marketing plan — a list of names. Temples, bhajana groups, Veda schools, family WhatsApp groups. Recommendation: one trusted introducer per community beats any amount of advertising for a devotional product, and the beta's 20–50 households should be chosen to become those introducers.
What happens if it works?
Ten thousand households is a part-time job you did not apply for: mail, corrections, requests, bills. Decide in advance what you will do — bring in a second pair of hands, cap growth, or accept the load — so that success does not arrive as a crisis.
What happens if you stop?
The unglamorous, genuinely ethical one. People will keep a year of practice in this app. Decide now that the corpus and the export format are open enough that the work survives you, and say so in the colophon. It also makes every other decision easier.

What this costs to run

Orders of magnitude, not quotes — the point is that the numbers are small, and that the shape of the bill is bandwidth, not compute. Static files plus a tiny database is the cheapest architecture there is.

ScaleMonthly, roughlyWhat actually costs money
Beta · 50 householdsFree to negligibleEverything fits inside free tiers. Domain is the only real line item.
Launch · 1,000 householdsLow tens of dollarsHosting and CDN egress, a small managed database, a transactional-mail allowance.
Working · 10,000 householdsLow hundredsSame, plus push volume — and, if audio has shipped by then, audio egress becomes the largest single line by a distance.
Two cost traps to avoid by construction: never put hymn text or audio behind a compute layer that charges per request when a CDN would serve it for free, and never bundle audio into the offline cache by default. Also, hold one month of the bill in reserve before you open the doors — dāna is unpredictable, and an app that goes dark for an unpaid invoice loses more than it saves.

What to measure, and what to ignore

You are not building a growth product; the wrong metric would push you to make it one. Three numbers matter.

  • Return on day seven. Of those who opened once, how many opened again a week later? For a daily-practice app this is the whole verdict. Everything else is decoration.
  • Recitations completed per returning household per week. Whether the app is in the practice, or merely installed near it.
  • Corrections reported, and days to fix. Your trust metric. A rising report count with a falling fix time is a healthy product; the reverse is a warning.

Deliberately ignore: installs, page views, session length (longer is not better — a reciter who finds their hymn in ten seconds is well served), and anything resembling a leaderboard. Keep the promise you already made: count actions, never people.

Your next three weeks, in order

The launch plan owns the fortnight to the beta. This is what to do around it, and none of it is coding — which is exactly why it gets skipped.

This week
Paperwork and honesty
Book the accountant conversation about the entity. Register the domain and the name. Then walk the app and label or cut every prototype so nothing in the beta lies: sign-in, giving, reminders, the print meter, audio.
Next week
The corpus ledger
One row per text: source, rights basis, proof status, reviewer. It is a boring afternoon that closes your largest legal risk and tells you exactly how narrow "launch narrow" has to be.
Week three
Ask the two questions only people can answer
Approach one or two reviewers for the corpus, and one introducer per community for the beta list. Both are slow, both are free, both are worth more than any feature.
Then
Pick the platform and start the port
After the beta has opened and told you something. Not before — the beta's findings are the port's brief.

What could actually kill this

K1 · most likely
You run out of energy before you run out of scope
One builder, an infinite corpus and no deadline is the classic shape of a beautiful unfinished thing. Ship narrow, on a date, and let the roadmap absorb the rest.
K2
A wrong text, found publicly
Reputation in this audience is built on accuracy and lost in a single screenshot. Proofing, named reviewers and a fast correction door are the whole defence.
K3
Silent data loss
Browser storage evicted, a year of practice gone, no backup and no export. It will happen to someone before you build sync. Warn now, fix in stage 2.
K4
A rights complaint about an edition or translation
Unlikely, survivable, and entirely avoidable with the sourcing ledger.
K5
Nobody comes back
The one risk that is information rather than failure. If day-seven return is low after a fair beta, the answer is to change the product, not to market harder.

A mentor's note

Three things worth hearing, since this is your first product.

The hard part is behind you, and it is not the part you think. Most first products fail because they have no reason to exist and no taste. Yours has a clear reason, a real audience and a distinct aesthetic — those cannot be bought. What remains is plumbing: laborious, well-documented, and solvable in a defined number of weeks. That is a very good position to be in.

The prototype's job is finished the day it teaches you something. Do not defend it, extend it, or fall in love with it. Get it in front of fifty households, learn what they actually do, and then build the real thing knowing what matters.

Ship less than you want to, sooner than feels safe, and tell the truth about the gaps. Every serious problem on this page is either "no back end yet" or "not decided yet". Neither is a crisis. But a prototype that pretends to be a product spends trust you cannot buy back — and for an app people will open before their morning prayer, trust is the whole asset.
