Blurb: Śiva's account of the river, spoken to Viṣṇu, and built around one very sharp claim: what a person sees when he looks at the Gaṅgā depends on what he has done. Those whose sin has been shaken off see the supreme light, equal to a thousand suns; the faithless, "whose eyes are damaged by sin," see an ordinary river full of ordinary water (vv. 10–11). The same water, two sightings. Around that the māhātmya piles up its comparisons — sin goes as darkness goes at sunrise, as serpents go at Garuḍa's glance, as a mountain of cotton goes at a single spark — and then makes the most generous statement in the whole Gaṅgā literature: on her bank, every hour is auspicious, every place is auspicious, and every person is a fit recipient of a gift (v. 13), which cancels at a stroke the entire ritual calculus of choosing the right moment, the right site and the right recipient. Its most affecting passage is the song the ancestors are said to sing forever, over and over, in all their many forms: will someone, anyone, in our line go and bathe in the Gaṅgā and offer us a handful of her water (vv. 20–22)? And its strangest is vv. 26–27, where sixty thousand guardian troops keep the unworthy away from the river by shooting them with arrows made of their own desire, anger and greed.
