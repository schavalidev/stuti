Title: Devī Catuḥṣaṣṭyupacāra-pūjā Stotram — the Goddess worshipped with sixty-four services
Devanāgarī: देवी चतुःषष्ट्युपचारपूजा स्तोत्रम्
Telugu: దేవీ చతుఃషష్ట్యుపచారపూజా స్తోత్రం

Author: Ādi Śaṅkarācārya. The colophon printed at the end of the text names him in the usual full form — "the work of the venerable Śaṅkara Bhagavat, disciple of the venerable Govinda Bhagavatpāda, of the paramahaṁsa-parivrājaka order" (iti śrīmatparamahaṁsaparivrājakācāryasya śrīgovindabhagavatpūjyapādaśiṣyasya śrīmacchaṅkarabhagavataḥ kṛtau devī catuḥṣaṣṭyupacārapūjā stotram).

Independent collation: against the printed **Catuḥṣaṣṭi Upacāra Pūjā of the Śrīguru Madanānanda Sarasvatī Pīṭham** (user-supplied scan, 19 pp.), which is a genuinely separate lineage — a maṭha's order of service, not a stotra anthology. Every verse checked against it was word for word the same. This is a real collation and not the same site in another script.

Recension note: the two witnesses are the same poem in two different forms, and neither is a defective copy of the other.

1. **stotranidhi prints the verses only, and says so** — its own header reads `ఈ స్తోత్రము (శ్లోకాలు మాత్రమే)`, "this stotra (the ślokas only)". That is the text given here.
2. **The Pīṭham edition sets the same verses out as a rite of sixty-four numbered services.** After each service's verse or verses it prints a long address to the Goddess — `श्रीमहाकाळी महालक्ष्मी महासरस्वती बालात्रिपुरसुंदरी राजराजेश्वरी भवानीचंद्रमौळीश्वर देवताभ्योनमः` — followed by `<service> समर्पयामि`, sixty-four times over. That refrain, and the service names, are not in the base text and are not reproduced here.
3. **What the Pīṭham edition adds besides.** Dhyāna verses of its own at the head, which stotranidhi does not carry; a sixteen-name **aṅga-pūjā** at its twenty-eighth service, running `ओं महाकाळ्यै नमः — पादौ पूजयामि` through `ओं सर्वदेवस्तुतायै नमः — सर्वांगानि पूजयामि`, with a direction to worship further with the Lalitā Sahasranāma, the Triśatī and the Khaḍgamālā; roughly seven closing ślokas after its sixty-fourth service, among them `या देवी मधुकैटभप्रमथिनी` and `मंत्रहीनं क्रियाहीनं`; and a closing prose formula. None of that is in the base text and none has been added to this file.
4. **The service numbers do not map one-to-one onto the verses, and the correspondence was checked rather than assumed.** It holds for long stretches — the Pīṭham's services 5 to 15 stand against verses 5 to 15 — but its twentieth service takes two verses, 19 and 20, and the count realigns after it. Its sixteenth service, the upavīta, has no verse in the base text at all. The sections below therefore group the verses by what they actually offer, which is visible in the verses themselves, rather than by that edition's numbering.
5. **Two printing errors in the Pīṭham edition, recorded and not carried over.** Its sequence marks run (0) to (64); the mark (55) is skipped entirely, and its `चतुरंगसैन्यम्` is marked (57) where the surrounding order requires (47).

Accent: none, and none is to be added. This is a stotra, and the base text prints it unaccented, as stotra is printed. The Pīṭham edition heads each of its sixty-four services with one ṛc of the **Puruṣa Sūkta** and one of the **Śrī Sūkta**, taken in order and **accented throughout**; those are Vedic text, they are not part of this poem, and they are not transcribed here. The corpus holds both sūktas accented already — see `../lakshmi/06_sri_suktam.txt` and the Puruṣa Sūkta files under `../../veda/`. Per the rule in the repo-root `CLAUDE.md`, accent is transcribed or absent, never inferred, and never moved from one text onto another.

Verse count: 72 verses. The text is called the worship "with sixty-four services", and the number of verses is not sixty-four. Sixty-nine verses carry the worship itself, and the last three are the phalaśruti. The count is reported as the text yields it and has not been trimmed to match the title; some single services take two verses, which is where the difference lies.

Blurb: This poem worships the Goddess by serving her through a whole day. She is woken at dawn by the songs of bards. She is led into a jewelled hall, seated on a throne, and given water for her feet. She is anointed with campaka oil and saffron, bathed, dressed, and given her ornaments one by one — anklets, girdle, necklace, armlets, bangles, earrings and the crest-jewel. She is offered sandal paste, flowers of many names, incense and lamps. She is given food, fruit and betel. Then she is offered a parasol, a mirror, a horse, an elephant, a chariot, a fourfold army, and the whole earth as her fortress. Dancers dance and the gandharva maidens sing at her door. At the close she is asked to rest, and to dwell always in the worshipper's heart. The worshipper who cannot perform the rite may recite the poem instead, and the last verses say that this brings the same fruit. Much of the poem is a list of named substances — flowers, spices, grains and fragrances — so it is also a record of what a South Indian household once had to hand for worship.
