Title: Nārāyaṇīyam — Daśaka 1: the greatness of the Lord
Devanāgarī: श्रीनारायणीयम् – प्रथमस्कन्धपरिच्छेदः – प्रथमदशकम् – भगवन्महिमानुवर्णनम्

Blurb: The hundred daśakas begin not with a story but with an argument. Bhaṭṭatiri states the Vedāntic definition of Brahman — dense with bliss and awareness, beyond comparison, unbounded by time or place, sounded out by a hundred thousand Vedic sentences and still not made plain — and then says that this very Brahman is standing in plain sight at Guruvāyūr, and what luck for the world that it is. Everything that follows rests on that first stroke. The daśaka then asks the question that the whole poem answers: if the absolute is formless, what is this body of dark beauty, and why is it worth worshipping? The reply is that the pure sattva of which the form is made is not a veil over Brahman but its clearest medium, like the crest of a wave on a still sea. The poet closes by taking the word bhagavat apart — lordship, power, fame, beauty, knowledge, detachment — and showing that each of the six belongs to the Lord of Guruvāyūr first and to everyone else by loan. The poem was begun as an act of healing: Bhaṭṭatiri had taken on his teacher's paralysis and composed a daśaka a day before the image until he was cured, which is why the second verse speaks of the hurt of the world and of holding fast with a steadier mind.

--- verse 1 ---
deva:
सान्द्रानन्दावबोधात्मकमनुपमितं कालदेशावधिभ्यां
निर्मुक्तं नित्यमुक्तं निगमशतसहस्रेण निर्भास्यमानम्।
अस्पष्टं दृष्टमात्रे पुनरुरुपुरुषार्थात्मकं ब्रह्मतत्त्वं
तत्तावद्भाति साक्षाद् गुरुपवनपुरे हन्त भाग्यं जनानाम्॥
iast:
sāndrānandāvabodhātmakamanupamitaṁ kāladeśāvadhibhyāṁ
nirmuktaṁ nityamuktaṁ nigamaśatasahasreṇa nirbhāsyamānam|
aspaṣṭaṁ dṛṣṭamātre punarurupuruṣārthātmakaṁ brahmatattvaṁ
tattāvadbhāti sākṣād gurupavanapure hanta bhāgyaṁ janānām||
en: That reality of Brahman whose very nature is dense bliss and awareness, which has no likeness, which is released from every limit of time and of place, which is eternally free, which a hundred thousand Vedic sentences go on declaring and still leave unclear — that same reality, the moment it is simply seen, becomes the great goal of human life itself. And that is what is shining in visible form at Gurupavanapura. What good fortune this is for the people of the world. — The opening verse settles the ground of the whole poem. The definition is the strict Vedāntic one, and nothing in it is softened; the turn comes in the last line, where what the Veda could not make plain is said to be standing in front of the eyes at Guruvāyūr. The exclamation hanta is the poet's own astonishment, not a figure of speech.

deva:
एवं दुर्लभ्यवस्तुन्यपि सुलभतया हस्तलब्धे यदन्यत्

en: When a thing so hard to reach has come easily to hand in this way, and a man still serves something else with his body, his speech or his mind — that, plainly, is nothing but pettiness. As for us, with a mind made steadier, and for the ending of the world's affliction, we take refuge in this very Lord of Gurupavanapura, who is the Self of all without remainder. — The word for the world's affliction is meant literally. Bhaṭṭatiri had taken his teacher's paralysis upon himself and was composing before the image in order to be rid of it; the poem's aim is stated here, in the second verse, and never repeated.

--- verse 3 ---
deva:
सत्त्वं यत्तत्पराभ्यामपरिकलनतो निर्मलं तेन तावद्
भूतैर्भूतेन्द्रियैस्ते वपुरिति बहुशः श्रूयते व्यासवाक्यम्।
तत्स्वच्छत्वाद्यदच्छादितपरसुखचिद्गर्भनिर्भासरूपं
तस्मिन् धन्या रमन्ते श्रुतिमतिमधुरे सुग्रहे विग्रहे ते॥
iast:
sattvaṁ yattatparābhyāmaparikalanato nirmalaṁ tena tāvad
bhūtairbhūtendriyaiste vapuriti bahuśaḥ śrūyate vyāsavākyam|
tatsvacchatvādyadacchāditaparasukhacidgarbhanirbhāsarūpaṁ
tasmin dhanyā ramante śrutimatimadhure sugrahe vigrahe te||
en: The sattva that is unmixed with the other two strands, and therefore spotless — out of that, and out of the elements and the senses made of it, your body is formed: so Vyāsa's words are heard again and again. And because that stuff is so clear, the supreme bliss and awareness within it is not covered at all but shines out through it. In that form of yours, sweet to hear of and sweet to think of, and easy to take hold of, the fortunate take their delight. — This is the poem's defence of image-worship, and it is an argument, not a plea. Pure sattva does not hide what is behind it; it transmits it. Hence the form is not a step down from the formless but the one place where the formless is legible. Note sugraha, easy to grasp: the point is accessibility, and it answers the first verse's aspaṣṭa, not made clear.

--- verse 4 ---
deva:
निष्कम्पे नित्यपूर्णे निरवधिपरमानन्दपीयूषरूपे
निर्लीनानेकमुक्तावलिसुभगतमे निर्मलब्रह्मसिन्धौ।
कल्लोलोल्लासतुल्यं खलु विमलतरं सत्त्वमाहुस्तदात्मा
कस्मान्नो निष्कलस्त्वं सकल इति वचस्त्वत्कलास्वेव भूमन्॥
iast:
niṣkampe nityapūrṇe niravadhiparamānandapīyūṣarūpe
nirlīnānekamuktāvalisubhagatame nirmalabrahmasindhau|
kallolollāsatulyaṁ khalu vimalataraṁ sattvamāhustadātmā
kasmānno niṣkalastvaṁ sakala iti vacastvatkalāsveva bhūman||
en: In that spotless ocean of Brahman — unshaken, forever full, having the form of the nectar of boundless supreme bliss, and most beautiful with the many strings of the liberated dissolved within it — the purest sattva is said to be like the rising of a wave. You are that sattva itself. Why then should you not be without parts? The statement that you have parts holds good only of your partial descents, O Infinite One. — The image turns on a pun that will not carry over: muktāvali is both a row of pearls and a company of the liberated, so the sea is adorned by the very souls that have dissolved in it. The argument answers an objection: if the Lord has a body he must be divisible. No — the wave is not another thing than the sea, and the divisions spoken of in scripture belong to the avatāras, which are portions, not to him.

en: Unborn one, though you have no activity at all, you take up, for no reason whatever, that act which is called looking. By that alone, at the beginning of a new age, nature rises again — nature that had lain dissolved and was as good as non-existent. And taking up a certain wholly pure portion of that nature, a portion of the form of sattva which conceals nothing, you wear the Vaikuṇṭha form, unhindered in the majesty of your own greatness. — Three words carry the doctrine. You have no work to do, so creation is not a need; there is no cause, so it is not owed to anyone; and the whole activity is only a glance. What the glance stirs is not a second reality but the prakṛti that was already yours, lying dissolved.

en: Your lordship governs even lords such as Śaṅkara. Your valour puts out the splendour of those who put out the splendour of the world.
