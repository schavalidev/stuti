Blurb: Three separate happenings are set side by side in this daśaka, and the same question runs through all of them. Devakī asks to see the six infants Kaṁsa killed, and the Lord goes down to Sutala, brings them up and sends them to his own abode. Then, at one and the same hour, he takes two forms and accepts worship in two houses in Mithilā — a king's, heaped with wealth, and a poor brahmin's, with fruit and boiled rice gathered that morning — and gives the two men the same release. The long middle of the daśaka is the story the colophon names, of the brahmin at Dvārakā whose children die one after another, of his bitter words, and of Arjuna's vow to enter fire if he cannot save the child. Arjuna's arrows and his yogic search through the cities of the gods come to nothing, and the Lord then takes him west past Lokāloka, through the darkness, to a place beyond all darkness where the children were being kept. The poet says plainly what he thinks the whole affair was for: to take away Arjuna's pride, and to lift a mind that had grown used to treating the Lord as a friend and a man. The last two verses step back from the story to Nārada, to Vasudeva's instruction and to Uddhava at Badarī, and then close with the prayer that this incarnation, in which men reached the deathless state by love and even by hatred, may end the world's affliction.

en: Your mother Devakī had long been hearing how you brought back your teacher's son, and a wish rose in her to see her own six children. At her word you went to Bali in the land of Sutala, and there Bali worshipped you. Those six had first been the sons of Marīci; by Brahmā's curse they were born as sons of Hiraṇyakaśipu, and then they were born of Vasudeva and killed by Kaṁsa. You brought them, showed them to your mother, and then led them to your own abode. — The daśaka opens on the far side of a grief that the tenth book left unclosed. The children Kaṁsa killed are not lost; they were under a curse, the curse is now spent, and the Lord is simply returning them.

en: After that, at Dvārakā, a brahmin's son died, and the brahmin spoke bitter words. You bore both, saying only, "Who indeed can hold back destiny?" I think it was done for two reasons: to take away Arjuna's pride, and to set his understanding, which had grown blunt from regarding you as a man, firmly upon the truth, by letting him see the supreme abode. — The poet does not report the reason; he says manye, I think. The verse is his own reading of an event that looks, on its face, like indifference.
