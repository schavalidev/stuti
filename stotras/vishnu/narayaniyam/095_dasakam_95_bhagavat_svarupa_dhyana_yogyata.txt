Blurb: This daśaka is the last of the teaching given to Uddhava, and it turns from instruction to practice. It begins far back, with the Lord taking the body of Hiraṇyagarbha at the start of creation and seeming to enter the condition of a living being, and it ends with a single worshipper seated in one place and holding his mind still. Between the two stands the argument of the whole daśaka: that devotion alone purifies the mind, that no amount of austerity or learning does this work without it, and that a mind so purified becomes fit to meditate on the form of the Lord. The turn comes at the seventh verse, where the poet stops describing and begins to do — the seat, the breath, the lotus of the heart turned upward, and then the dark and tender form placed upon it. The eighth and ninth verses give that form limb by limb and then gather the whole attention into one place, the smiling face, until the mind rests in unbroken bliss. The tenth verse refuses the powers that such meditation brings, because they delay the meeting, and asks instead only for the Lord himself and for protection from all affliction.

deva:

जीवत्वं प्राप्य मायागुणगणखचितो वर्तसे विश्वयोने।
तत्रोद्वृद्धेन सत्त्वेन तु गुणयुगलं भक्तिभावं गतेन

iast:
ādau hairaṇyagarbhīṁ tanumavikalajīvātmikāmāsthitastvaṁ
jīvatvaṁ prāpya māyāguṇagaṇakhacito vartase viśvayone|

cchittvā sattvaṁ ca hitvā punaranupahito vartitāhe tvameva||
en: Source of the universe, at the beginning you take on the body of Hiraṇyagarbha, which holds every living being within it without exception. Then, having come to the state of a living being, you remain as though studded with the whole set of the strands of māyā. There, by sattva that has grown strong and has turned into devotion, the other two strands are cut; then sattva itself is given up, and freed of every limiting adjunct I come to exist as you yourself, you alone. — The daśaka opens by placing the individual soul's whole course inside the Lord himself. The three strands are dealt with in order: rajas and tamas are cut by a sattva made strong through devotion, and sattva in its turn is set aside, because it too is a limit. What is left is not a new state but the original one, and in it the worshipper exists as the Lord himself.

en: O boundless one, sometimes, when sattva rises, a man does come to know the fault in the taste of sense-objects. Even then, when rajas along with tamas grows strong, his turning again towards those objects is hard to stop. For the mind and the strands are knotted together here, each with the other. To hold all of them back, single devotion to you, who are the fourth and beyond the three, is the only refuge. This you taught, taking the form of the swan. — Knowing the fault is shown to be not enough by itself. The reason is given plainly: mind and strands are tied to each other, so the mind cannot untie them. The teaching in the form of the swan was given to Brahmā and to the sons of Brahmā, and it is recalled here as the authority for what the verse says.

en: Kṛṣṇa, many means of good have been laid down for those who perform rites, according to the difference of their liking. From those means many kinds of goal are reached, but the joy in them is small and it comes to an end. To your friend you declared that of all these goods devotion alone is the greatest. And indeed, by what could those who enjoy sense-objects obtain a delight equal to the bliss of devotion to you? — The friend is Uddhava, to whom the whole of this teaching is given. The verse does not deny the other means; it measures them. They work, they are graded to different tastes, and what they give is small and ends.

en: O Lord, placing my mind, full of eager delight, again and again upon all your limbs, I then fix it upon one place among them — the beautiful lotus of your face with its gentle smile.
