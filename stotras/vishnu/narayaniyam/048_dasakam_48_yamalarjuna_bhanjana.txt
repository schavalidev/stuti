Blurb: This daśaka tells the release of the two sons of Kubera, who had been cursed to stand as a pair of arjuna trees in Vraja. It opens with the gods praising the Lord as Dāmodara, the one with the rope at his waist, while he sits bound to the mortar and sees the two trees standing near. The middle of the daśaka is the story behind those trees: Nalakūbara and Maṇigrīva, made proud by the wealth they had won through the worship of Śiva, were sporting naked in the Gaṅgā with many women when Nārada came upon them. The women covered themselves; the two were too drunk to notice him. Nārada cursed them, but the curse was made for their good — he says plainly that without peace there is no happiness, and he fixed the end of their tree-life at the sight of the Lord. The child then drags the mortar between the two trees and brings them down, and two beings of great brilliance step out of them and praise him. The eighth verse draws the lesson the poet wants: these two were servants of Rudra, and even so they came in time to the feet of the Lord and asked for nothing but the highest devotion. The daśaka closes with the cowherds carrying the child home, saying it is only by the power of Hari that he was not crushed, and with the usual prayer for relief from disease.

en: The hosts of gods, filled with great gladness, called out the name Dāmodara and praised you with joy. You, soft of belly, stayed bound to the mortar at your ease, and saw two arjuna trees standing not far off. — The daśaka takes up exactly where the last one left the child, tied to the mortar. The name Dāmodara, he who has the rope at his waist, is given by the gods at the moment the binding happens, so that what looked like a punishment becomes a title. The last words turn the story outward: the child sees the trees, and the rest of the daśaka follows from that look.

--- verse 2 ---
deva:
कुबेरसूनुर्नलकूबराभिधः परो मणिग्रीव इति प्रथां गतः।

en: One son of Kubera bore the name Nalakūbara, and the other was well known as Maṇigrīva. Made proud by the wealth they had obtained through the worship of Maheśa, they turned away from you and sported for a long time. — The verse gives the cause of everything that follows. Their wealth was rightly earned, by the service of Śiva; it was the pride in it that turned them away. The poet does not blame the worship, and this matters for verse 8, where the two are still called servants of Rudra.

deva:

en: In the river of the gods the two of them, swollen with pride, were surrounded by many young women who had drunk wine and were singing. They were without clothing and given over to their sport, and Nārada, who is turned to your feet alone, saw them there. — The verse turns on a play of sound: surāpagā is the river of the gods, and surāpa is one who drinks wine. The poet sets the two words side by side so that the holiness of the place and the state of the company stand against each other in one line. Nārada is described by his devotion, not by his anger, and the description prepares the curse that follows.

en: Out of fear the women there put on their clothes at once. These two, whose minds were blinded by pride, saw the sage standing before them and still did not do so. Then the sage pronounced his word upon them, so that devotion to you and inward peace might be attained by them. For without peace, from where can happiness come? — The last line is the reason for the curse and is stated as a general truth, not as an accusation. The curse is called a means to devotion and to peace, which is how the poet wants it read: a sage who is turned to the Lord alone can intend nothing else by it.

--- verse 5 ---
deva:
युवामवाप्तौ ककुभात्मतां चिरं हरिं निरीक्ष्याथ पदं स्वमाप्नुतम्।
इतीरितौ तौ भवदीक्षणस्पृहां गतौ व्रजान्ते ककुभौ बभूवतुः॥
iast:
yuvāmavāptau kakubhātmatāṁ ciraṁ hariṁ nirīkṣyātha padaṁ svamāpnutam|
itīritau tau bhavadīkṣaṇaspṛhāṁ gatau vrajānte kakubhau babhūvatuḥ||
en: "You two shall take on the nature of arjuna trees for a long time. Afterwards you shall see Hari, and then you shall regain your own place." Spoken to in this way, the two of them came to long for the sight of you, and became a pair of arjuna trees at the edge of Vraja. — The curse carries its own end within it, which is the mark of a sage's curse in this literature. The word for their state changes from a punishment to a waiting: they long for the sight that will release them. They are placed at the edge of Vraja, so that the child's play may reach them.
