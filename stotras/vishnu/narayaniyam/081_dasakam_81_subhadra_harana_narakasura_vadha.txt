Devanāgarī: श्रीनारायणीयम् – दशमस्कन्धपरिच्छेदः – एकाशीतितमदशकम् – सुभद्राहरणं कालिन्द्यादिविवाहवर्णनं नरकासुरवधवर्णनं च

Blurb: This daśaka covers a long stretch of story in ten verses, and it moves quickly. The first half is marriage after marriage. Satyabhāmā is still being fondly cared for when the Lord goes with her to Draupadī's wedding, stays a while at Hastināpura for Arjuna's sake, has Indraprastha built, and returns. Then Subhadrā, his own younger sister, whom Duryodhana was asking for, is carried off by Arjuna in the disguise of a mendicant at the Lord's own word, and Balarāma's anger has to be talked down. Kālindī is seen on the bank of the Yamunā and taken; Mitravindā is seized from the midst of the kings; Satyā of Kauśala is won by taking seven bodies at once and binding seven bulls in a moment; Bhadrā is given by her brothers; Lakṣmaṇā of Madra is won by piercing the fish that Arjuna and the rest could not. That makes eight chief queens, and the count is stated plainly. The second half turns to war. Garuḍa is remembered and comes, Satyabhāmā is carried on the Lord's lap, Prāgjyotiṣa is made into a city of blood, Mura of the five faces and then Naraka himself are cut down, and sixteen thousand women held captive are sent to Dvārakā, the Lord's own city. The daśaka ends with the journey to heaven to return the earrings, the taking of the wish-granting tree, and the poet's own explanation of the quarrel with Indra: it was done to show what the fault of wealth looks like.

en: Garuḍa, the best of birds, came the moment he was remembered. You mounted him, carried Satyabhāmā on your lap, and went to the enemy's city as though to a pleasure garden. The name of the city and the word for blood are set beside each other at the end of the verse, and the play is the poet's.

deva:

en: Mura of the five faces sprang at you from the middle of the watery forest. His head was split at once by you with the discus. Then Naraka, who was kindling the battle with his four-tusked lordly elephants, was cut down by that same discus, and you made him one who had crossed beyond hell. — Tīrṇanarakam is a play on the demon's name: by being killed by the Lord, Naraka crosses naraka. The verse does not say he was destroyed but that he was carried across, which is how this poem treats those the Lord kills.

en: Praised by the Earth, you at once gave his kingdom and one elephant to Bhagadatta, the son of Naraka. The captive women are described by their devotion before anything else is said of them.

deva:
भौमापाहृतकुण्डलं तददितेर्दातुं प्रयातो दिवं

bhaumāpāhṛtakuṇḍalaṁ tadaditerdātuṁ prayāto divaṁ

en: You went to heaven to give back to Aditi the earrings that Bhauma had carried off. There Indra and the other gods honoured you, together with your beloved, whose beauty put the women of heaven to shame. You took the wish-granting tree, and when Indra fell upon you in anger you defeated him and came away. But that you did only in order to show what the fault of wealth looks like. — The last line is the poet's own reading of the quarrel, and it protects both parties: Indra's anger is the fault of prosperity, and the Lord's victory is an explanation rather than a triumph.
