Title: Śrī Lakṣmīnṛsiṁha Aṣṭottaraśatanāma Stotram
Devanāgarī: श्रीलक्ष्मीनृसिंह-अष्टोत्तरशतनामस्तोत्रम्
Telugu: శ్రీ లక్ష్మీనృసింహ అష్టోత్తరశతనామ స్తోత్రం

Author: Anonymous; the colophon of both stotra and nāmāvalī places the text in the **Nṛsiṁhapūjākalpa** — a ritual manual for the worship of Nṛsiṁha, not a Purāṇa. This is the first entry in this workstream whose stated source is a kalpa rather than a Purāṇa or a Tantra.

Source / recension: Primary text: **Gītā Press, Gorakhpur — Śatanāma Stotra Saṅgraha**, code 1850, pp. 48–51 (stotra pp. 48–49, nāmāvalī pp. 50–51), transcribed from the page images of the printed edition rather than from OCR. Entry 7 of the volume's forty. Both colophons read `इति नृसिंहपूजाकल्पे`. Stotra and nāmāvalī are paired in one file as the publisher prints them, and were checked against each other name by name: the stotra lists the 108 in the nominative, the nāmāvalī re-inflects them into the caturthī, and the two run in exactly the same order with no divergence and no gap — the cleanest internal agreement of the seven entries done so far. Checked against the twenty existing files in `narasimha/`: no aṣṭottaraśatanāma was present, so this is the folder's first, but **the dhyāna verse is already in the corpus** and gave a genuine second witness — see the recension note. `विकर्त्रे` (no. 43), `भूतोत्थ` (no. 81) and `रञ्जित` in the dhyāna were re-rendered at 500 dpi. No web witness was sought for the stotra proper.

Recension note: **Prefix.** Gītā Press prints `ॐ` before each name; this corpus prints `श्री` for every nāmāvalī without exception (user, 2026-09-08). **One divergence, and it comes from inside the corpus.** The single dhyāna verse here is the opening stanza of the Lakṣmīnṛsiṁha Karāvalamba Stotram, already held as `narasimha/01_lakshminrsimha_karavalamba_stotram.txt` v. 1 (from stotranidhi). The two witnesses differ at one syllable: Gītā Press prints `भोगीन्द्रभोगमणिरञ्जित-` where stotranidhi prints `भोगीन्द्रभोगमणिराजित-`. Both are good Sanskrit and both are widely circulated — *rañjita* "made lovely, coloured" and *rājita* "made resplendent" say nearly the same thing of the same gems. **Gītā Press is followed here**, per the standing rule, and the reading was verified at 500 dpi; `narasimha/01` keeps its own reading, and the two files now cross-reference. Gītā Press also sets the verse as two long lines where stotranidhi sets four, and splits `निकेतन चक्रपाणे` where stotranidhi writes the compound closed. **Shape of the text.** The shortest entry of the seven: one dhyāna, fourteen name-verses, one phalaśruti — 16 blocks against the 88 of Baṭuka Bhairava. There is no frame, no viniyoga, no nyāsa and no speaker; the hymn opens on the names in the second block and closes six lines later. **All 108 names are distinct** — no repetition anywhere, the second file after Veṅkaṭeśa to manage this, and here at the canonical count rather than at 109. **Nominative stotra, dative nāmāvalī**, the ordinary arrangement of this volume (Veṅkaṭeśa, entry 6, was the exception). **`वैशाखशुक्लभूतोत्थः` (v. 11, no. 81) is left glossed conservatively.** The obvious reference is Nṛsiṁha Jayantī, Vaiśākha śukla caturdaśī, and `भूत` is read as a number-word for the fourteenth tithi in some quarters; but as a bhūta-saṅkhyā *bhūta* standardly means five, not fourteen, so the English column says only "who arose in the bright fortnight of Vaiśākha" rather than asserting a tithi the compound does not unambiguously give. **`अघोर` (no. 21) is printed `चाघोरो` in v. 3** with the `च` of the sandhi absorbed; the nāmāvalī's `अघोराय` settles the name. **`शिंशुमार` (no. 45)** is the celestial dolphin, the constellation-form of Viṣṇu, and is retained as printed rather than normalised to `शिशुमार`.

Blurb: A hundred and eight names for the god who came out of the pillar, and almost nothing else — no frame, no ritual apparatus, six lines of hymn on either side of the list. The names keep circling the moment of the avatāra: the blazing face, the fang that is his weapon, the claws that are his weapons, the eye hard to look upon. Then, at the end, the turn the story always makes — the same terrible one is the protector of Prahlāda.
