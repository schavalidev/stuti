Independent collation: **a second printed edition of the whole rite**, supplied by the user — *Śrāvaṇa Maṅgaḷagaurī Vrata Kalpamu*, written with an Āndhra tātparya by **Challa Lakṣmī Nṛsiṁha Śāstri**, printed at Machilipatnam in **1958** (33 pp., 400 copies, priced four annas). That is a different editorial lineage from a stotra site, seventy years older, and it is the stronger printed authority of the two. It carries no text layer and was read from the page images.

Recension note: **the two editions are not two copies of one text. They are two different works for the same vrata, and this file is the first of the two.**

1. **They agree on the frame, and the agreement is real.** Both open with the same second resolve and both fix the same term for the vrata — **the five years beginning with the first year of marriage**. Both give the same nineteen names of the aṅgapūjā in the same order, from Umā at the feet to Maṅgaḷagaurī at the whole body. Both point at the hundred and eight names rather than printing them. Both close with the binding of the thread and the giving of the vāyana to married women whose husbands are living. Two lineages agreeing on all of that is worth stating plainly.
2. **They differ wholly in the verses used at the sixteen services, and this is not reconciled.** The base text's services are carried by the long verses of the **Lalitā ṣoḍaśopacāra** set, in the śārdūlavikrīḍita metre — `kallolollasitāmṛtābdhilaharīmadhye`, `hrīṁkārāṅkitamantralakṣitatano`, `hrīṁkāreśvari taptahāṭakakṛtaiḥ`. The 1958 edition prints short verses in the anuṣṭubh metre instead, with its own Telugu tātparya after each — `payodadhighṛtaṁ caiva madhuśarkarayā samam`, `jāhnavītoyamānītaṁ śubhagandhaprasaṁyutam`. The meditation verse differs likewise. **Neither set is a defective copy of the other and neither has been used to fill a gap in the other.**
3. **The story is a different story.** The base text tells it in **Telugu prose**: Dharmapāla the merchant, the ascetic who will not take alms in a childless house, the single mango, the son Vāradatta who is short-lived, the princess Suśīlā whose mother had kept this vrata, the serpent bound in the pot that turns into a necklace of pearls, the defeat of the messengers of Yama, and the sight restored to the blind parents-in-law. The 1958 edition tells its story in **Sanskrit ślokas with Telugu tātparya**, spoken by **Kṛṣṇa to Yudhiṣṭhira**, set in **Kuṇḍinanagara**, and turning on a Brahmin, an ascetic and the release from a curse. **There is no overlap between the two narratives at all.** Per the rule in the repo-root `CLAUDE.md`, a difference that runs through the whole text is a second file and not a bracketed variant. **The 1958 recension is therefore work to follow, as its own file, and this file does not stand in for it.**
4. **One bracketed variant, and it is the source's own.** At the lamp the base text prints `[రత్న]` beside `మణిమయస్తంభేషు`, marking the other reading `रत्नमय` for `मणिमय`. It is written into the line in brackets, which is how this corpus prints variants.
5. **The closing verse of the offering of flowers already stands in this corpus.** `सर्वमंगळ मांगळ्ये शिवे सर्वार्थ साधिके` is the well-known verse of the Durgā Saptaśatī and closes the Nārāyaṇī Stuti at `../../devi/durga/34_narayani_stuti.txt`. Both are kept and cross-referenced; the tradition catalogues them separately.

Not collated: the hundred and eight names. The rite points at two nāmāvalis rather than printing them — the one of Maṅgaḷagaurī and the one of Lalitā — and lets the worshipper choose. **Neither is in this corpus yet**; they are work to follow, not part of this file. The base text also points at a hymn to Maṅgaḷacaṇḍikā at the prayer, which is likewise not in the corpus.

Blurb: This is the vrata of Maṅgaḷagaurī, kept by married women on the Tuesdays of the month of Śrāvaṇa. It is kept for five years, beginning with the first year of marriage, and it is kept for two things: that the husband may live, and that there may be sons, grandsons, wealth and good fortune. The goddess is Gaurī, the wife of Śiva, worshipped here as sixteen years old with her youth unchanging. The sixteen services are offered to her, and at each one a long verse is said and then a short one. The nineteen limbs of the goddess are then worshipped by name, from the feet upward, and the hundred and eight names are said. A thread of five strands is tied upon the right hand. The story is told in nine parts. A merchant's son is born short-lived, and his life is saved by his marriage to Suśīlā, in whose mother's house this vrata had been kept. On the night of the marriage the goddess appears to Suśīlā in a dream and tells her to bind the serpent in the pot her mother had given her; the serpent is found next morning to be a necklace of pearls. Later the goddess is seen in a dream fighting the messengers of Yama and defeating them. The rite closes with the giving of the vāyana to married women whose husbands are living, which is the act the whole story turns upon.

The first meditation. The goddess is seen anointed with saffron, her forehead touched with musk, holding the arrow, the bow, the noose and the goad — the four weapons of Lalitā. **The verse is one of the ṣoḍaśopacāra verses of the Lalitā worship, and the whole of this edition's sixteen services is built from that set.** Where the second edition prints short verses in the anuṣṭubh metre, this one prints these long ones.

vidhi:
The prayer. Whoever worships the daughter of the mountain with the garland of the letters of her mantra gains a clear mind.
