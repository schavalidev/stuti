Blurb: The Vedic mantras are recited only by one for whom the upanayana saṁskāra has been performed. This form of the rite leaves them out, and where the rite needs a verse in their place, the Paurāṇika verse is given. The worship so performed is complete. The form with the Vedic mantras is kept separately, for those who have had the upanayana. This is the vrata of Maṅgaḷa Gaurī. It is kept on every Tuesday of the month of Śrāvaṇa, not on one day only, and it is kept every year from the first year of the marriage to the fifth year. It is kept by the newly married woman herself. The goddess is made ready in turmeric or in gold, and the life-breath is called into that image. She is then meditated upon as a girl of sixteen whose youth never passes, and the sixteen services are offered to her, from the water for the feet to the waving of lights. Her limbs are worshipped by eighteen names, from the feet upward to the head. The hundred and eight names are then recited; the text prints no list and lets the worshipper use the names of Maṅgaḷagaurī or the names of Lalitā. A thread of five strands is bound on the right hand. The story is told after the worship. In it a merchant named Dharmapāla, long childless, is given a son who is to die young. The son, Vāradatta, sets out for Kāśī while he is still alive, and on the way he is married to Suśīlā, a girl whose mother kept this vrata. On the night of the marriage the goddess comes to Suśīlā in a dream and tells her to shut the serpent that has come for her husband inside the earthen pot her mother had given her as the vāyana. Suśīlā does it. Her husband is taken away to Kāśī before dawn, and she refuses a second marriage and waits for him, worshipping the goddess and feeding travellers, until he returns and is known by his ring. The rite ends with the prayer, the asking of pardon, and the giving of the vāyana to married women whose husbands are living.
