Blurb: Rāvaṇa speaks for a whole sarga, and never once threatens her. He begins by telling her not to be afraid and saying that though seizing another's wife is the rākṣasas' own dharma, he will not touch her against her will. Then he attacks her mourning itself as unbecoming: the single braid, the bed of earth, the soiled cloth, the fasting without cause. In their place he offers garlands and sandal, divine ornaments, song and dance, the rank of chief queen over all his women, the jewels crushed out of the worlds, the whole earth conquered and handed to her father for her sake. The argument he keeps returning to is time: her youth is passing, and what has passed does not return, like the swift current of water. Then he turns to Rāma, and every sentence diminishes him — clad in bark, his splendour gone, sleeping on bare ground, perhaps not even alive, and in any case unable to reach her. The sarga ends with him asking her to walk with him through the flowering woods by the sea.
