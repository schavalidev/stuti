Blurb: Hanumān leaps from the rampart into the aśoka grove, and for a while the sarga is pure spring. Sāla and campaka and mango in flower, cuckoos and bees, peacocks crying. He wakes the sleeping birds, and the trees they start from shed their blossom until he stands covered in flowers like a hill made of them, and every creature that sees him running through the grove takes him for Spring itself. Then the same force turns destructive: the trees he shakes stand stripped like gamblers who have lost their clothes, and the grove is left with its finest trees broken. He goes on past floors of gem and silver and gold, step-wells with stairs of jewel and sand of pearl, a river falling from a hill like a woman who has sprung from her lover's lap. At last he finds a single śiṁśupā tree of gold, ringed with golden altars, and climbs it. From there, he reasons, he will see her: she was always one who loved the woods, she will come to the river at dusk for her evening rite. So he hides in the thick leaves and waits.
