Blurb: A whole sarga of deliberation, and one of the most practical passages in the poem. Hanumān has heard everything and now has to decide how to approach her. If he leaves without comforting her she will die before he returns; if Rāma asks what Sītā said he will have nothing to report. But he is a vānara, and very small, and if he speaks Sanskrit like a brahmin she will take him for Rāvaṇa in disguise and cry out — and then the guards will come, and he may be caught or she may be killed, and the whole embassy fails. He weighs his own strength honestly: he could kill thousands of rākṣasas, but he could not then carry her across a hundred yojanas of sea. The line he settles on is the sarga's centre: battles are uncertain, and what wise man turns a task that is free of doubt into one full of doubt? He also repeats the maxim from sarga 2, that a confused messenger ruins a settled purpose. His solution is to speak in a human tongue but softly, and to begin not with himself but with Rāma, as her own kinsman.
