Blurb: Sītā answers, and the sarga takes its name from what she does before she speaks. She lays a blade of grass between herself and Rāvaṇa, and addresses him across it, so that she never speaks to him directly at all. Her argument is first moral and then political. Other men's wives are to be guarded as your own; make yourself the measure. A king of unformed self destroys prosperous kingdoms, and Laṅkā with all her jewels will perish through the fault of one man. Then she offers him the way out, and it is a generous one: make Rāma your friend, give me back, and it will be well with you, for he loves those who come to him for refuge. If not, the arrows are coming, and she describes them falling on the city like serpents with blazing mouths. Her last charge is the sharpest. He did not fight Rāma for her. He waited until both brothers had gone out and took her from an empty hut, because he could no more stand in their sight than a dog in the sight of two tigers.
