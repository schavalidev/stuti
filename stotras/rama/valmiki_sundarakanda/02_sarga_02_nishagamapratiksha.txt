Blurb: Hanumān has crossed. He comes down on Trikūṭa and finds that a hundred yojanas have cost him neither breath nor weariness, and he tells himself he could cross many hundreds more. Then he looks at what he has come to. The sarga is largely one long seeing: the flowering woods and tanks and gardens of the island, and then the city itself, walled in gold, moated, towered, flagged, built by Viśvakarman and held by Rāvaṇa, so high that it seems to float in the air. The poet describes the city as a woman, her rampart the hips and the deep moat her fresh garment. What Hanumān draws from the sight is not wonder but a soldier's judgement. Laṅkā cannot be taken by war, not even by the gods, and of all the vānaras only four could reach it at all. Conciliation, gift and division are equally useless against these rākṣasas. So he sets the question aside until he knows whether Sītā is alive. He reflects that a task is ruined by a messenger who loses his head, and that even a settled plan fails if it is set against the place and the hour. He decides to wait for dark, shrink himself to the size of a cat, and go in alone. The sarga ends with the sun set and the moon rising white as a conch, like a swan upon a lake.
