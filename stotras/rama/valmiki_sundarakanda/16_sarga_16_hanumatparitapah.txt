Blurb: Having found her, Hanumān grieves. The sarga is his lament, and it works by adding up what her loss has already cost. For her sake Vālī was killed and Kabandha struck down, Virādha slain, fourteen thousand rākṣasas destroyed at Janasthāna with Khara and Triśiras and Dūṣaṇa; for her sake Sugrīva gained the kingship of the vānaras, and for her sake he himself crossed the sea. If Rāma were to overturn the earth and the world besides for her, that too would be fitting: the kingship of the three worlds would not equal one part of Sītā. Then he recalls who she is, the daughter who rose from a furrow when Janaka's plough broke the earth, and what she gave up, following her husband into an empty forest and finding the same joy there as in a palace. The line the sarga turns on is plain: the husband is a woman's highest ornament, higher than ornament itself, and parted from him she does not shine, though she deserves to. He ends by saying that Rāma does a hard thing merely in staying alive.
