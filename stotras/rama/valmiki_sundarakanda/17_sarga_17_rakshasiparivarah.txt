Blurb: The moon rises clear and lends Hanumān its light, as though helping him. By it he sees first not Sītā but her guards, and the poet spends fourteen verses on them. They are catalogued by deformity: one-eyed, one-eared, earless, with ears like pegs; with noses set crosswise or breathing from the forehead; with the faces of boars and tigers and sows and camels; with long tongues and nails, smeared with flesh and blood, holding iron spears and clubs, quarrelsome and always drinking. Against that ring of shapes the poet sets Sītā beneath the tree, and every image of her is of something dimmed rather than deformed: a star fallen when its merit is spent, a cow-elephant parted from her herd, the crescent behind autumn cloud, a lute nobody has touched, a lotus-fibre smeared with mud that shines and does not shine. Her one remaining ornament is her husband's love, and her one guard is her own virtue. Hanumān sheds tears of joy at the sight, bows to Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa, and hides himself again.
