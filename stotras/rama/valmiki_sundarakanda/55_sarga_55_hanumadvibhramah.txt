Blurb: Laṅkā is burning, and Hanumān is suddenly afraid of what he has done. The sarga is almost entirely his own thought. He says that blessed are those who hold back anger by their understanding, that an angry man may kill even his elders, and that one who casts off anger by forbearance is like a serpent casting off its old skin. He calls himself shameless and the greatest of sinners, because the whole city is burnt and Sītā was in it. He asks whether he should throw himself into the fire or into the mouth of the submarine fire, because he cannot go back alive having destroyed the work. He follows the loss out to its end: if Sītā has died, Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa will die, Bharata will not survive the news, and the house of Ikṣvāku will fall. Then his reasoning turns. Fire is not fit to touch the wife of that righteous one. Her own truthfulness and devotion to her husband protect her. He remembers Mount Maināka rising in the sea to help him, and takes it as evidence of what such power can do. Good omens then appear, and he hears the vidyādhara women saying that the city is burnt but Sītā is unharmed, and he is at peace.
