Blurb: This sarga is written in a long metre from beginning to end, and it turns on a single absence. It opens with eight verses on the risen moon, compared in turn to a bull in its pen, a swan in a silver cage, a lion in a cave, a hero on an elephant, and a white mountain with one high peak. Under that light Hanumān goes through the houses of Laṅkā. He sees the drunk quarrelling and boasting and flinging their arms about, women asleep beside their husbands, women anointing themselves, laughing, angry, weeping. He sees rākṣasas at their study and rākṣasas of fearful shape, and their wives among them, shining like stars. The poet gives a long account of their beauty, rows of faces bright as the moon, rows of eyes, rows of ornaments like lightning. Then the sarga turns. In all of it he does not find Sītā. The four verses that describe her are a chain of images of something faint and hard to see: a crescent barely made out, a line of gold covered with dust, the scar of a healed arrow wound, a line of cloud torn by the wind. Not finding her, Hanumān is struck with grief.
