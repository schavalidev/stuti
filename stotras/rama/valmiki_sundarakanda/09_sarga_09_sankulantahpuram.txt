Blurb: Hanumān enters the inner apartments and finds a house half a yojana across. He climbs into the Puṣpaka, and the poet pauses to say how Rāvaṇa came by it: Viśvakarman made it for Brahmā, Kubera won it by austerity, and Rāvaṇa took it from Kubera by force. A scent of food and wine draws Hanumān on like a kinsman calling him, and leads him to the great hall where Rāvaṇa sleeps. Most of the sarga is that hall at the middle of the night. A thousand women lie asleep on the carpet, overcome by drink after their play. Their faces are lotuses closed for the night, their ornaments have slipped and their garlands turned, their breath stirs the ends of their garments above their faces. They lie across one another's arms and laps until the poet calls them one garland strung on the thread of their own arms, or a grove of creepers twined together by the wind. Hanumān reasons about what he sees, and then the sarga turns. Not one of these women was taken by force; every one came willingly. Only Sītā was carried off. He thinks that if Rāma's wife were like one of these, it would be well — and then corrects himself. Sītā is higher than all of them, and what the lord of Laṅkā has done to her is an ignoble act.
