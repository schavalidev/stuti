Blurb: The longest of the despair sargas, and the one that reasons furthest. Hanumān first runs through the ways Sītā may have died: fallen from Rāvaṇa's arms in flight, her heart broken at the sight of the sea, crushed by his grip, eaten by him or by his wives. Then he thinks about telling Rāma, and follows the consequence step by step into a chain of deaths — Rāma will die of the word, then Lakṣmaṇa, then Bharata, then Śatrughna, then the three mothers, then Sugrīva, then Rumā and Tārā and Aṅgada, and then the vānaras by poison and hanging and fire and the sword. So he will not go back. He will live at the root of a tree, or build a pyre, or enter the water. Then he checks himself again: in destruction there are many faults, and a living man sees good things. He will hold to life, because if he lives, union is certain. And there is one place he has not searched. The sarga ends with him bowing to Rāma and Sītā and to the gods by name, and asking the question the whole kāṇḍa has been asking: when shall I see that face?
