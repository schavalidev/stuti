Blurb: Eight verses, and the hinge of the kāṇḍa. Sītā stands with the cord in her hand, and the signs come. Her left eye throbs, dark and wide and ringed with curving lashes, like a lotus struck by a fish. Her left arm trembles, the arm her husband once lay upon. Her left thigh quivers, and tells her that Rāma is standing before her. Her golden garment slips a little. In the Indian reading of omens, the left side stirring is auspicious for a woman, and these are the same signs Trijaṭā had already pointed out to the rākṣasa women. The poet compares her reviving to seed that was withered by wind and sun and is brought back by rain. Her face, with its bimba-red lip and white teeth, shines again like the moon released from the mouth of Rāhu, and the sarga ends with her grief gone and her fever quieted.

en: Upon her in that state, distressed, blameless, her joy gone and her mind most wretched, auspicious omens came, as dependants come to a man blessed with fortune.
