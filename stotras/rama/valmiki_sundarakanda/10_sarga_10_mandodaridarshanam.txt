Blurb: Hanumān finds Rāvaṇa asleep. The sarga looks at him slowly and in parts. First the crystal couch under a white parasol, fanned by women with yak-tail fans. Then the sleeper himself, dark as a raincloud, red-eyed, breathing like a serpent, so that Hanumān draws back in fear and watches from an alcove. Then the two arms alone are given ten verses: scarred by the tusks of Airāvata, furrowed by Indra's thunderbolt, cut by the discus of Viṣṇu, flung out on the white couch like two five-hooded serpents. The wounds are the record of who he has fought. Around him the women of the household sleep where the night left them, each holding the instrument she played — a vīṇā clasped like a boat in a flood, a small drum held like an infant, a flute embraced like a lover met in secret. Then Hanumān sees a woman lying apart on a couch of her own, golden, beautiful, mistress of the household, and concludes from her beauty that this must be Sītā. He is wrong. It is Mandodarī. In his joy he slaps his arms, kisses his tail, sings, climbs the pillars and tumbles down, showing, as the poet says, the nature of vānaras.
