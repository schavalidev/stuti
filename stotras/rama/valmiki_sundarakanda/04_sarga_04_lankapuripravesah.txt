Blurb: Laṅkā has let him pass, and Hanumān goes in. He does not use a gate. He leaps the rampart, and as he comes down he sets his left foot on the head of his enemies, which is how a conqueror enters a city. Then he walks the great road, and the sarga becomes a record of a city at night heard rather than seen. Laughter and shouting, instruments, the singing of women flushed with wine, girdles and anklets, feet on stairs, men slapping their arms and calling out challenges. In the houses of the rākṣasas he hears mantras being muttered and finds night-rangers at their study, and others roaring the praise of Rāvaṇa. Then he comes upon the guard. The poet lists them at length: consecrated men with matted hair or shaven heads, armed with handfuls of darbha grass, with clubs and pikes and thunderbolts; men with one eye or one ear, hunched or dwarfed or misshapen, and others fair and bright. A hundred thousand of them stand unshaken before the inner apartments. Past them, on the crest of the mountain, stands the house of Rāvaṇa, moated and walled and loud with horses and elephants. Hanumān enters it.
